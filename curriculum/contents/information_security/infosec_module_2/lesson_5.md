# Risk Communication & Reporting

## Why Communication Matters

A risk assessment buried in a spreadsheet helps no one. Security professionals must communicate risks clearly to decision-makers, technical teams, and non-technical stakeholders — in language each audience understands.

---

## The Risk Register

A **risk register** is the central document for tracking identified risks. It captures:

| Column | Description |
|--------|-------------|
| **Risk ID** | Unique identifier |
| **Description** | What is the risk? |
| **Asset** | What is affected? |
| **Likelihood** | Low / Medium / High |
| **Impact** | Low / Medium / High |
| **Rating** | Combined risk level |
| **Owner** | Who is responsible? |
| **Treatment** | Accept / Avoid / Transfer / Reduce |
| **Control** | What is being done? |
| **Status** | Open / In Progress / Closed |

---

## Communicating to Different Audiences

### Executives / Board
- Focus on **business impact** and **financial exposure**
- Use high-level summaries: "Our three highest risks are…"
- Connect to regulatory penalties or reputational harm

### Technical Teams
- Share specific vulnerability details, affected systems, and remediation steps
- Use technical language freely

### General Staff
- Focus on what they should **do differently**
- Use plain language, avoid jargon
- Phishing awareness, password hygiene, reporting suspicious activity

---

## Effective Risk Reporting

A good risk report answers:
1. **What** is the risk?
2. **Why** does it matter (business impact)?
3. **What are we doing** about it?
4. **What do we need** (budget, people, time)?

---

## Security Governance

Risk communication feeds into **security governance** — the structure by which an organization directs and oversees its security program. This includes:
- Security committees and steering groups
- Regular reporting cadence (monthly, quarterly)
- Escalation paths for critical risks
- Board-level accountability (CISO, CRO)

Strong governance ensures risk decisions are made intentionally, not by default.
